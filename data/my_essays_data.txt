Morality of suicide and the limits of the autonomy
Voluntary Euthansia is mostly identify as suicide, and that is probably the best description you could give to it. But, does this self-determination morally justify someone else to kill that person? Dan.W. Brock argues that euthanasia is morally permissible based on two values: Autonomy and the individual well-being. Individuals should have the freedom to choose to make decisions about their own lives, including in the moments of suffering or hopeless cases. In cases where life is considered no longer a benefit but instead a burden for the patient, euthanasia can be considered to relieve suffering. This if we take only the autonomy argument. On the other hand, Daniel Callahan argues against Active Euthanasia/physician-assisted suicide. Callahan believed permittering legal euthanasia will bring a dangerous precedent of “consenting adult killing”, introducing possible problems and contradictions such with the role of medicine, which should focus on healing and alleviating suffering, not to cause death. He also mentions the slippery slope argument, where if society accepts euthanasia as an ethical option, it might go beyond what was originally its initial scope, almost like dominos pieces. He feared this could potentially allow non-voluntary or involuntary euthanasia, and could possibly end protections for vulnerable individuals, such as those with disabilities. Not only that, it's also mentioned how if we allow euthanasia based only on personal suffering or preferences it could make life-ending decisions more subjective and harder to regulate. Since it will be open to interpretation, it will be allowing potential abuse of the law.

Killing vs Allowing to Die
Some philosophers like J. Gay-Williams thinks that passive euthanasia is morally better than active euthanasia, since if we take the definition of euthanasia as “The intentional or deliberate killing of a person, typically to relieve suffering”, we therefore conclude passive euthanasia is not euthanasia, since it does not directly involve killing someone. Others, such as James Rachels, explain these are basically the same. PE is often morally acceptable, while AE is always wrong, but both of these are done intentionally and have the same end. Rachels explains those with the Smith–Jones scenario where one actively drowns his cousin while the other just lets him drown to his own death. Rachels argues that both actions are equally morally reprehensible because the intent and outcome are the same. Active euthanasia can often be more humane than passive euthanasia. For example, withholding treatment might prolong suffering, active euthanasia could end it quickly and painlessly.

He did believe there was enough technology at the time to perhaps send a crewed rocket to the Moon, …but not enough technology developed to return the crew safely to Earth.
